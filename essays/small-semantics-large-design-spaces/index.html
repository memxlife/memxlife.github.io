Verified Freedom in Tensor Computing

Toward a Constructive Theory of Software–Hardware Co-Design

Tensor computing has become the computational foundation of modern AI, but its systems are still designed through abstractions inherited from a different computational era. The general-purpose stack was organized around stable instruction interfaces and programs whose future control, memory accesses, and parallelism often became known only as they ran. Modern AI exposes a different structure: bulk operations, multidimensional dependencies, and data reuse are frequently visible before execution, while a narrower class of uncertainty—shapes, request arrivals, routing, latency, and failure—remains genuinely dynamic.

This change invites more than another compiler or accelerator. It invites a constructive theory of tensor computing: a model that connects what an AI program means to how software schedules it, how hardware realizes it, and how physical resources constrain it.

Such a theory must not make the system artificially static. Hardware organization is fixed at manufacture. Many mappings are fixed during compilation. Concrete shapes and load may become known only at launch. Batching, allocation, routing, and scheduling may still need to adapt while the system runs. The problem is to place each decision at the stage where its information becomes available, then make the remaining runtime freedom both useful and efficiently verifiable.

The hypothesis is that this structure can separate two complexities that general-purpose systems often entangle: a vast space of implementation choices and a smaller, typed space of runtime behaviors that correctness must cover. Establishing that hypothesis creates two connected scientific problems. At the computation layer, we must discover which tensor-computing restrictions make the separation real. At the implementation layer, we need a model that preserves software and hardware physical priors across abstraction boundaries, so decisions made at manufacture, compile, launch, and runtime can be optimized together without breaking correctness.

We propose a proof-carrying co-design model for tensor computing. Typed, compositional contracts connect program meaning, software decisions, hardware resources, runtime actions, implementation refinement, and measured physical cost. Because proposal is separated from acceptance, the model can safely support powerful search—including LLM-powered agents, reinforcement learning, Bayesian optimization, evolutionary methods, and conventional solvers—without treating statistical confidence as correctness.

The central question is:

Can tensor computing expose a large cross-layer design space, retain the runtime adaptation needed for efficiency, and still make every accepted transformation, implementation, and runtime action verifiable?

The foundational bet has a direct falsifier. On matched workloads and resources, if the runtime freedom needed to approach the unrestricted performance frontier cannot be captured by a sound envelope whose checks are small relative to the actions being optimized, then this direction fails.

1. From instruction-level parallelism to tensor computing

Instruction-level parallelism was an organizing problem of the general-purpose era. The processor sees a stream of scalar instructions, but future branches, addresses, and dependencies may not be known early enough to schedule all useful work statically. Out-of-order execution moves that discovery into hardware. Tomasulo-style reservation stations, tags, and register renaming encode dependency discovery and scheduling in runtime state [R7]. Later processors add speculation, recovery, and increasingly elaborate memory-system state.

This does not make CPUs incorrect or impossible to verify. It explains why a high-performance implementation admits many histories that are absent from the architectural program. Verification at design time must account for those histories because it cannot know which branch outcomes, addresses, cache events, arbitration outcomes, or latencies a future workload will produce.

Tensor computing begins with a different object. A dense matrix multiplication, convolution, or attention region exposes a multidimensional iteration space and regular producer–consumer relations. The main performance question is often not how to discover independent work, but how to map known work onto finite compute, storage, and communication resources. Many mapping choices can be fixed early; others remain valuable because they respond to shapes or load.

The design space must not be confused with the accepted or deployed system:

$$ \mathcal D_{\mathrm{deployed}}(P) \subseteq \mathcal D_{\mathrm{certified}}(P) \subseteq \mathcal D_{\mathrm{candidate}}(P). $$

An optimizer explores candidate designs. A checker admits the certified subset. Deployment selects one or a few certified designs. For a deployed design \(z\), let \(\mathcal A_z(s)\) be the candidate runtime actions exposed in state \(s\):

$$ z\in\mathcal D_{\mathrm{deployed}}(P), \qquad a_t\in\mathcal A_z(s_t), \qquad s_{t+1}=T_z(s_t,a_t,\eta_t). $$

Here \(\eta_t\) contains external events. The geometry of \(\mathcal D_{\mathrm{candidate}}(P)\) governs design search. The action set \(\mathcal A_z(s)\) governs runtime adaptation. The states reachable under those actions govern explicit-state verification. Unselected hardware designs are not runtime states, but a runtime scheduler still creates alternatives inside the selected design.

The mathematical separation is elementary; the engineering claim is not. We must show that the candidate space remains rich, that \(\mathcal A_z(s)\) retains the adaptation needed for competitive performance, and that its effects can be checked without exploring an uncontrolled state space. Search cost, certificate construction, and any family-wide proof may still scale with the candidate space; separating them from runtime state does not make them free. The objective is not to move everything offline. It is to expose exactly where each kind of freedom belongs.

2. The verification opportunity in tensor computing

2.1 The useful restriction is stronger than “dataflow”

Synchronous dataflow gives the clearest causal example. If every firing of actor \(a\) produces and consumes a fixed number of tokens, then the occupancy of channel \(e\) after \(k\) firings is

$$ b_e(k) = b_e(0) + \sum_a \Gamma_{e,a}N_a(k). $$

Here \(\Gamma_{e,a}\) is the fixed token-rate contribution of actor \(a\) to channel \(e\), and \(N_a(k)\) is its firing count. Buffer safety can therefore be reduced to arithmetic over firing counts; tensor payload values do not enter the occupancy equation. This conservation law does not by itself prove that a feasible, bounded, deadlock-free schedule exists; fixed-rate graphs admit separate static schedule and buffer analyses for those obligations [R10].

Dataflow alone is insufficient. Kahn process networks are deterministic in their stream meaning, yet determinacy does not imply finite buffers or a statically bounded schedule [R11]. Parks showed that termination and bounded scheduling are undecidable for the general model [R12]. The tractability comes from restrictions such as fixed or controlled rates, affine iteration domains, bounded resources, disciplined effects, and explicit timing—not from drawing the program as a graph.

Prior work establishes several separate pieces. Polyhedral models turn affine iteration and dependence questions into integer relations and support verified code generation [R13]. Pure tensor languages support compositional proofs of schedule rewrites and lowering [R14, R15]. Verified high-level synthesis shows that an untrusted scheduler can propose a high-performance schedule while a small validator checks it [R16]. Wavelet verifies compilation to asynchronous dataflow through forward simulation, then proves determinacy and deadlock freedom separately; one correct schedule does not establish that every legal schedule is correct [R31]. TrainVerify provides direct modern evidence that tensor size need not determine equivalence-checking cost: its verified shape reduction scales distributed-training plan checks to Llama 3 405B and DeepSeek-V3, although it does not verify kernels, buffers, runtime protocols, or hardware implementation [R25]. None of these results alone proves that a complete system for tensor computing is easy to verify. Together they identify a plausible restricted region.

We call such a region a certified region of tensor computation. Its contract states:

  • the tensor operations and numerical observation relation;
  • execution geometry—shape extents, active index sets, routes, and work counts;
  • token rates, dependencies, and memory effects;
  • resource capacities, routes, conflicts, and backpressure rules;
  • latency assumptions and the decisions fixed before runtime; and
  • the residual events that may still vary during execution.

The contract earns its name only when these facts are checked, not merely declared.

2.2 Payload abstraction requires a proof

Write an execution state as \(s=(q,\gamma,v)\), where \(q\) contains control and resource state, \(\gamma\) contains execution geometry, and \(v\) contains tensor payloads. Geometry includes extents, active index sets, routes, and work counts; it may be fixed for a region or may evolve. A useful abstraction has the form

$$ \alpha_z(q,\gamma,v) = \bigl(q,\gamma,\rho_z(v)\bigr), $$

where \(\rho_z(v)\) is a declared bounded summary of the payload facts that can affect control. Let \(S_{0,z}\) and \(\widehat S_{0,z}\) be the concrete and abstract initial states, and let \(\varphi\) and \(\widehat\varphi\) be the corresponding property interpretations. A sound abstraction must cover initial states, simulate transitions on reachable well-formed states, and preserve the property being proved. Let \(h_z\) map each concrete event label to its declared abstract observation:

$$ \begin{aligned} \alpha_z(S_{0,z}) &\subseteq \widehat S_{0,z}, \\ s\xrightarrow{\lambda}_z s' &\Longrightarrow \alpha_z(s) \;\widehat{\xrightarrow{h_z(\lambda)}}_z\; \alpha_z(s'), \\ \widehat\varphi\bigl(\alpha_z(s)\bigr) &\Longrightarrow \varphi(s). \end{aligned} $$

The abstract transition may over-approximate the concrete one, but proving the property on the abstract system must imply it for the concrete system [R28, R32]. Setting \(\rho_z(v)=\bot\) only erases payloads syntactically. To preserve both control state and observable actions, define

$$ \mathcal Q_z(s) = \left\{ \bigl(h_z(\lambda),\alpha_z(t)\bigr) \;\middle|\; s\xrightarrow{\lambda}_z t \right\}. $$

Payload erasure is an exact quotient only if states with the same abstraction induce the same labeled abstract successors:

$$ \alpha_z(s_1)=\alpha_z(s_2) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathcal Q_z(s_1)=\mathcal Q_z(s_2). $$

Exact quotienting is sufficient, not necessary; bounded summaries and conservative over-approximation may also be sound. Hardware data-independence work gives checkable semantic conditions under which control does not depend on particular data values [R33]. It is direct precedent, but its restrictions exclude equality-dependent control and arbitrary tensor arithmetic.

This makes the claim measurable rather than verbal. Every field of \(\gamma\) and \(\rho_z\) needs declared provenance, and the abstraction and precision must be fixed before comparison. A linear summary can still collapse an exponential payload-value space, so summary size alone is not the falsifier. The relevant test is scaling: if abstract reachability, certificate size, or checking time grows with payload volume enough to erase the claimed advantage, the hypothesis fails.

Fixed shape is not sufficient: MoE routing can change active work and routes while keeping tensor extents fixed. Value-dependent sparsity, addresses, retries, and early termination can do the same. Numerical correctness remains a separate symbolic or equational proof over \(v\). Concurrent events may be collapsed only after proving that they preserve one another’s enabledness and commute for the chosen observation; partial-order reduction does not follow from parallelism alone [R17]. Likewise, repeated tiles require an induction, cutoff, or assume–guarantee rule before one local proof can cover the whole array.

The number of explicit states is therefore one useful measurement, not a complete theory of verification cost. Certificate size, solver complexity, inductive invariants, numerical semantics, and implementation refinement must also be measured.

2.3 Dynamic shape is sometimes a parameter and sometimes state

Fixed shape is also not necessary. Shape is only one part of execution geometry. A shape known at runtime does not destroy the opportunity if the resulting geometry is fixed at the boundary of a tensor region rather than evolving as part of its control. It can still select a different topology, schedule, and trace length, so immutability removes one source of temporal change but does not by itself bound proof complexity.

If an invocation receives geometry \(\gamma\) once, a parametric proof may cover a family \(\mathcal G\) without enumerating every concrete shape and route. For example, a parameterized schedule can establish both semantic refinement and buffer safety:

$$ \begin{aligned} \forall \gamma\in\mathcal G:\qquad& \operatorname{Exec}(z,\gamma) \sqsubseteq P_\gamma, \\ & \forall e,k:\quad 0\le b_e(k,\gamma)\le B_e. \end{aligned} $$

A runtime guard checks \(\gamma\in\mathcal G\); the symbolic certificate proves that every admitted geometry respects buffer capacity \(B_e\) for the supplied schedule. Schedule existence and progress remain separate obligations. The runtime value becomes an initial parameter if it remains immutable, but the cost of proving the whole family must still be measured. Size-dependent array types demonstrate that useful shape equalities can be checked parametrically, while StableHLO explicitly distinguishes shape polymorphism, bounded dynamism, unbounded dynamism, and data-dependent dynamism [R18, R19].

A guard is not enough if a launch-time compiler constructs a new mapping. Either the mapping generator must be proved for all \(\gamma\in\mathcal G\), or each generated mapping must carry a certificate checked before execution. An unbounded geometry also cannot satisfy one finite-capacity guarantee without an explicit rejection, partitioning, or spill contract.

Some dimensions evolve but remain structured. KV-cache length during decoding, for example, can often be modeled by a monotone recurrence under a declared maximum. Such a property may need induction rather than state enumeration. Ragged batches can remain parametric if their length vector is fixed at launch, although proof and scheduling cost may grow with the number of independent lengths. Systems such as PagedAttention show why the runtime layer still matters: KV storage grows dynamically and requires block allocation [R26].

The hard case is payload-dependent shape. The output size of a filtering operation, expert routing load, speculative-decoding acceptance, or sequence termination may depend on computed values. If those sizes change allocation, routing, synchronization, or loop bounds, shape has become control state, and a payload-free abstraction may fail or require a larger summary. Dynamic-network compilers already report that optimization must then account for possible paths and shapes [R20]. MegaBlocks makes the corresponding physical tradeoff concrete for MoE: dynamic routing otherwise forces token dropping or wasteful padding [R27].

Modern language-model systems contain all three cases. The promising object is therefore not one globally static graph. It is a hierarchy in which bounded dynamic mechanisms select, specialize, and connect parameterized certified regions of tensor computation. The research question becomes how much dynamism can be expressed through small typed contracts, and how much must remain in the verified state.

3. A constructive model of computation and implementation

Tensor structure alone does not expose the implementation space. A workload graph does not say which fusion is numerically legal, which tile fits a buffer, which route avoids a conflict, which hardware operation should exist, or how a change in memory banking affects clock period and energy.

Traditional intermediate representations solve an essential engineering problem: each layer receives a manageable contract. But lowering also commits choices. If one layer discards an alternative or hides a cost, another layer cannot recover it. Cross-layer co-design therefore needs neither one flat IR nor the removal of abstraction. It needs typed interfaces that preserve the facts on which another layer’s decision depends.

A model is constructive in this paper when every accepted computation has a path to a resource-bounded implementation, every abstraction edge states what must be preserved, and every remaining runtime choice is governed by a checked contract.

3.1 Physical prior and commitment stage

The hardware prior is the finite resource system:

  • compute units and supported operations;
  • memories, capacities, banks, ports, and lifetimes;
  • links, topology, bandwidth, routes, and locality;
  • latency, initiation interval, synchronization, and clock domains;
  • sharing, arbitration, conflicts, backpressure, and failure behavior; and
  • area, timing, energy, and reliability assumptions with provenance.

The software prior is the legal way computation can use that finite system: numerical rewrites, tiling and fusion, layout and placement, lifetimes, synchronization, scheduling, and the information a runtime policy may observe or act upon. Hardware defines possible resources; software structure defines which uses of them can be expressed and when a choice can be made.

Hardware design chooses which resources exist. Compilation transforms the computation and allocates known work. Launch-time specialization uses concrete shapes and the available machine. Runtime policy responds to information that arrives during execution. These are commitment stages over one physical system, not independent optimization problems.

One label is not enough: a decision may be constrained early but selected later. For every choice \(x\), the model therefore records

$$ \kappa(x) = \bigl( \kappa_{\mathrm{constrain}}(x), \kappa_{\mathrm{select}}(x) \bigr), \qquad \kappa_{\mathrm{constrain}}(x) \preceq \kappa_{\mathrm{select}}(x), $$

where the ordered stages are manufacture, compile, launch, and runtime. A runtime allocator, for example, is constrained by manufactured capacity but selects concrete blocks at runtime.

Moving a decision later may improve adaptation but can enlarge runtime state and checking cost. Moving it earlier can simplify execution but lose performance under changing conditions. Choosing the commitment stage is itself a cross-layer optimization.

Consider autoregressive decoding. Manufacture fixes compute arrays, memory banks, ports, and links. Compilation selects tensor layouts and kernel families. Launch reveals concrete sequence limits and available devices. Runtime forms batches and allocates KV-cache blocks as requests arrive. These choices share the same capacities and data movement, yet a conventional staged flow optimizes them through separate interfaces. In the proposed model, a change in memory banking must update the legal tile family, the launch-time capacity certificate, and the runtime allocation envelope through typed relations. This is what “cross-layer” means: not one optimizer making every decision at once, but one model preserving the consequences of each decision across stages.

3.2 Runtime optimization inside a verified freedom envelope

The model must not prescribe one complete static schedule. It must define the freedom within which a runtime optimizer may act.

Let \(I(s)\) be the invariant that captures semantic progress, dependency order, live storage, resource capacity, and other safety facts relevant in state \(s\). A runtime optimizer proposes action \(a\) with evidence \(\pi_a\). The trusted runtime checker defines the locally accepted subset:

$$ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Safe}_z(s,a) &\Longleftrightarrow \exists \pi_a:\; \operatorname{Check}_{\mathrm{rt}}(z,s,a,\pi_a) =\operatorname{accept}, \\ \mathcal A_{\mathrm{safe}}^z(s) &= \left\{ a\in\mathcal A_z(s) \mid \operatorname{Safe}_z(s,a) \right\}. \end{aligned} $$

Invariant preservation alone is too weak: a wrong computation or a permanent no-op may preserve buffer bounds. Let \(\sigma_z(s)\) be the corresponding specification state. The offline theorem for the checker must establish enabledness, safety, and action-level semantic simulation:

$$ \begin{aligned} & I(s)\land \operatorname{Check}_{\mathrm{rt}}(z,s,a,\pi_a) =\operatorname{accept} \\ &\qquad\Longrightarrow \operatorname{Enabled}_z(s,a) \\ &\qquad\quad\land\ \forall \eta\in\mathcal E_z(s,a),\ \forall s'\in\operatorname{Post}_z(s,a,\eta): \\ &\qquad\qquad I(s') \land \sigma_z(s) \xRightarrow{\ell(a,\eta)}_P \sigma_z(s'). \end{aligned} $$

Here \(\mathcal E_z(s,a)\) is the declared set of environmental events for which the guarantee is claimed. The weak labeled transition \(\xRightarrow{\ell(a,\eta)}_P\) means zero or more specification-internal steps, the declared observable label, then zero or more internal steps; an implementation-internal action uses \(\ell=\tau\). Depending on the semantics, an action may form a batch, select a certified kernel, allocate memory blocks, schedule independent tiles, choose a legal route, or adjust precision and power within explicit error and timing contracts.

Local acceptance is still not permission to compose actions arbitrarily. A sequence of individually safe actions can deadlock or starve. A runtime policy is admitted only when its complete traces refine the specification and meet a progress contract:

$$ \begin{aligned} R\in\mathcal R_{\mathrm{adm}}^z \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad& \forall\tau\in \operatorname{Traces}(z,R,\mathcal E_z): \\ & \sigma_z(\tau)\in\operatorname{Traces}(P) \land \operatorname{Live}_z(\tau). \end{aligned} $$

\(\operatorname{Live}_z\) may state fairness, bounded response, deadlines, and the required fallback after rejection or an out-of-envelope event. This is a supervisory-control problem: expose the largest useful set of actions whose closed-loop behavior stays within the legal language [R29]. A finite-prefix monitor can enforce safety and bounded-progress obligations, but not arbitrary liveness. General liveness therefore needs an offline policy proof, or it must be reduced to an enforceable ranking, deadline, or guaranteed fallback controller. The same condition rules out infinite internal stuttering.

At runtime, the system should not repeat whole-system model checking. The expensive proof establishes the checker and invariant offline. Compile- or launch-time specialization supplies certificates, while runtime performs small incremental checks over the affected dependencies and resources. Whether that check can remain cheap while \(\mathcal A_{\mathrm{safe}}^z(s)\) remains large enough for useful adaptation is one of the central questions of this research.

This architecture has precedents but not yet the required tensor-computing scope. Proof-carrying code separates an untrusted producer from a small proof checker [R23]. Shielded reinforcement learning separates a reward-seeking policy from a synthesized safety monitor [R24], and supervisory control asks for the maximally permissive legal behavior [R29]. None supplies the cross-layer resource, timing, numerical, and implementation contracts proposed here.

3.3 A proof-carrying co-design model for tensor computing

The certified regions introduced in Section 2 are the computation nodes of one typed contract graph. Each carries its tensor semantics, execution geometry, resource invariant, and runtime freedom envelope. Other nodes describe software construction \(C\), architecture \(H\), runtime policy \(R\), and lower implementation \(L\); their edges state compatibility or refinement with workload semantics \(P\). Thus a transformation or runtime action remains valid across layers only when every downstream node preserves the assumptions used to admit it. A DSL may construct this graph, but the scientific object is the model and its composition rules.

The trusted base consists of the contract semantics, proof kernels, runtime checker, and the mechanism that binds a deployed configuration to checked parameters. Agents, optimizers, schedulers, code generators, certificate producers, and physical cost models need not be trusted. Any timing, energy, or reliability fact obtained only by calibration remains an empirical assumption, not a theorem.

The architectural assumptions cannot be an informal list assembled after the proof. Let

$$ A=\operatorname{Req}(C,R) $$

be the typed functional, resource, temporal, and numerical requirements mechanically exposed by the operations used in \(C\) and \(R\). Lowering must satisfy contextual refinement:

$$ \begin{aligned} L\sqsubseteq_A H \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad& \forall K\in\operatorname{Ctx}(A): \\ & \operatorname{Obs}\bigl(K[L]\bigr) \preceq_{\mathcal O,\varepsilon} \operatorname{Obs}\bigl(K[H]\bigr). \end{aligned} $$

The observation preorder must account for accepted inputs and refusals, finite and infinite traces, output values and allowed numerical error, divergence, progress, and timing whenever higher layers rely on them. Restricting \(K\) to contexts that satisfy the typed assumptions makes the contract explicit. For the software and runtime stack, define \(K_{C,R}[-]\equiv\operatorname{Exec}(-,C,R)\). The required inference is

$$ \begin{aligned} K_{C,R} &\in\operatorname{Ctx}(A), \\ \operatorname{Exec}(H,C,R) &\sqsubseteq P, \\ L &\sqsubseteq_A H \\ &\Longrightarrow \operatorname{Exec}(L,C,R)\sqsubseteq P. \end{aligned} $$

This inference is valid only after proving that \(\sqsubseteq_A\) is a precongruence for the proposed typed contract language. Interface automata establish such substitutivity for a narrower untimed I/O setting [R30]; they are precedent, not a proof of the richer tensor relation. The \(\operatorname{Exec}(H,C,R)\) premise includes the policy-level runtime contract; contextual refinement prevents one layer from silently assuming behavior that the next does not provide.

3.4 Optimize common cases; prove every admitted case

Let \(\Pi=(C,H,R,L)\) denote a certified cross-stage candidate, \(\theta\) a workload instance, \(\mu\) the observed workload distribution, and \(J_\beta\) the cost predicted or measured by backend \(\beta\). Let an admitted external scenario \(\omega_{\mathrm{ext}}\in\Omega_{\mathrm{adm}}^{\mathrm{ext}}\) include payloads, geometry, and allowed environment events. Runtime choices, nondeterminism, rejection, and fallback remain internal behaviors quantified by \(\operatorname{Exec}\). The desired separation is:

$$ \begin{aligned} \underset{\Pi\in\mathcal D_{\mathrm{certified}}}{\operatorname{minimize}} \quad& \mathbb E_{\theta\sim\mu} \left[J_\beta(\Pi,\theta)\right] \\ \text{subject to}\quad& \forall\omega_{\mathrm{ext}}\in\Omega_{\mathrm{adm}}^{\mathrm{ext}}: \\ & \operatorname{Exec}(\Pi,\omega_{\mathrm{ext}}) \sqsubseteq_{\mathcal O,\varepsilon} P(\omega_{\mathrm{ext}}). \end{aligned} $$

Performance may be optimized statistically for the shapes and loads that occur in practice. Correctness is required for every admitted scenario, under named environmental and physical assumptions and the declared observation \(\mathcal O\) and numerical tolerance \(\varepsilon\). Formal refinement does not prove that the physical cost model \(J_\beta\) is accurate; latency, area, and energy claims still require calibration and measurement.

Hybrid Agentic AI may search offline designs, synthesize specialization rules, or learn runtime policies. An online optimizer may itself be heuristic or learned, but action checking alone is insufficient. Its policy must be proved admissible offline, or an enforceable monitor and fallback must keep its behavior inside \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{adm}}^z\). LLM-powered agents need not sit on a latency-critical path; they are one possible source of proposals, not the semantics or the authority for correctness.

4. An unfinished constructive program

Computing history matters here not as ornament, but because each foundational model made a previously informal boundary precise. The early pioneers did not possess one complete theory of hardware. Von Neumann wrote in 1948 that a satisfactory mathematical theory of automata was still distant [R5]. Their legacy is a method for building such a theory: identify the problem’s physical structure, choose the right abstraction, and preserve a path back to construction.

Lovelace separated operations, operands, and the mechanism because a general analytical engine had to be understood independently of one calculation [R1]. Turing made mechanical computation precise because “effective calculation” lacked a mathematical definition; universality deliberately abstracted away physical economy [R2].

Shannon faced the constructive problem: how can logical meaning be realized and simplified as a relay network? His two-way correspondence joined semantics, synthesis, and equipment cost [R3]. Von Neumann and his collaborators faced scarce equipment, storage, and reliability. They reasoned in terms of elementary organs, composition, and time–equipment tradeoffs, while insisting that a black-box axiom must ultimately be justified by the physical unit below it [R4, R5]. Wilkes then showed that one level’s hardware control could be implemented by a lower-level interpreter, making the software–hardware boundary movable [R6].

The stable ISA and dynamic ILP answered a later problem: preserve broad software compatibility while recovering performance across varied programs. Systolic and tensor architectures answered another: turn regular dependence and reuse into local communication and high throughput [R8, R9].

The lesson is not to imitate one historical machine. It is to model the structure of the new computational problem, preserve exactly the facts needed for realization, and make refinement and physical cost explicit. Synchronous dataflow, affine programs, verified tensor transformations, formal accelerator interfaces, timing-aware hardware types, and validated HLS schedules establish important parts of this program [R10, R13–R16, R21, R22]. Abstract interpretation provides sound state abstraction [R28]; supervisory control formalizes the largest safe closed-loop behavior [R29]; and interface automata make environmental assumptions and component guarantees compositional [R30]. The missing capability is their disciplined composition for tensor computing across manufacture, compile, launch, and runtime, while measuring useful freedom, checking cost, and physical accuracy together.

5. Research agenda: finding the boundary

The theory becomes meaningful only if it identifies where verification remains tractable without removing the freedom that produces efficient systems.

Question 1: Where is the verification boundary?

Construct matched regions of tensor computation with static geometry, launch-time symbolic geometry, bounded evolving geometry, and payload-dependent geometry. For each, measure reachable abstract state, certificate size, checking time, runtime branching, and physical performance. Freeze the property, abstraction map, and precision before comparison. The decisive result is whether useful dynamism can be represented by parameters or compact invariants rather than by payload-dependent state.

Question 2: How much runtime freedom can be verified cheaply?

For one fixed workload and machine, compare a static schedule, an oracle-admissible dynamic policy, and a runtime optimizer constrained by the checked envelope. Both dynamic policies must satisfy the same semantic and liveness contract; give them the same candidate actions and declare exactly what future information the oracle may see. Measure objective quality, reachable abstract state, check latency, certificate size, progress violations, rejection, and fallback. The envelope succeeds only if it recovers useful adaptation while checking remains small relative to the work being scheduled. A pilot estimates the acceptable objective gap and checker overhead, then freezes both margins before a confirmatory run.

Question 3: Does the cross-layer model preserve useful designs and proofs?

Define a small independent reference space for tiled matrix multiplication or attention. Compare a conventional staged interface with the proof-carrying model under exhaustive enumeration. Measure the accepted Pareto frontier and representation loss, not syntax count. For each accepted design, check computation preservation, resource and timing safety, runtime-envelope soundness, and contextual implementation refinement separately. A smaller architectural state is not a success if implementation proof dominates or if a held-out architecture requires hidden checker semantics.

Question 4: Can Agentic search exploit the accepted space?

Only after the representation is frozen, compare hybrid Agentic search, Bayesian or evolutionary optimization, and other baselines over the same accepted candidate space and evaluation budget. This tests search efficiency, not model expressiveness or correctness.

The direction is weakened or falsified if:

  • near-optimal systems for tensor computing consistently require unbounded or cheaply uncheckable runtime freedom;
  • dynamic execution geometry cannot be isolated behind compact parameters, invariants, or bounded runtime contracts;
  • runtime checking overhead removes the performance gained by adaptation;
  • proof cost grows with tensor payload or replication despite the claimed abstraction;
  • the contract graph offers no useful designs beyond a matched staged interface;
  • a held-out architecture requires new checker semantics rather than new parameters and contracts; or
  • physical predictions do not remain calibrated on held-out designs.

Current claim boundary

Existing work establishes several mechanisms used by this argument, but no result yet establishes their proposed composition. We do not yet know whether the verified runtime envelope is large enough, whether its checks are cheap enough, or whether the cross-layer model retains competitive physical designs. This paper defines the research direction and the evidence needed to decide it.

Conclusion

The opportunity is not that tensor computing is static or small. It is that its different forms of complexity may be separated and placed deliberately. Some choices belong to design search. Some become known at launch. Some must remain adaptive at runtime. They need not all become unchecked machine state.

Search space is not state space, but runtime state cannot be wished away. A constructive theory of tensor computing must expose a large cross-layer design space, define an efficiently verified envelope for necessary runtime freedom, and preserve meaning through physical implementation.

If that theory is possible, it establishes a new division of labor. Statistical intelligence explores designs and policies. Formal contracts govern what may be accepted. Physical measurement remains the authority on efficiency. This is the foundation required for Agentic AI to participate responsibly in the construction of future computing systems.
